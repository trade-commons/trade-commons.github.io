The Global Trade Commons exists to make open, verifiable digital trade infrastructure usable in
practice. It provides a neutral, multi-stakeholder home for the shared tools, tests, reference
implementations and registries that the UN/CEFACT digital trade standards, and the open standards
they build on, need in order to be adopted rather than merely published.

Particular attention goes to lowering adoption barriers for developing and emerging economies, so
that the benefits of open digital trade infrastructure reach the economies that would otherwise be
left behind.

## What is in scope

- Conformance and interoperability test harnesses for the UN/CEFACT digital trade suite, principally
  the UN Transparency Protocol (UNTP) and UN Verifiable Trade Documents (UNVTD), and for the open
  standards they build on.
- Reference implementations and shared tooling that lower the cost of adoption.
- Operating or supporting common registries and interoperability infrastructure as public goods.
- Documentation, guidance, worked examples and onboarding material for implementers.
- Adoption programmes, community activation and ecosystem coordination.
- Capacity building for developing and emerging economies.
- Fee-for-service conformity testing and registration, operated on a cost-recovery, open and
  non-discriminatory basis.

## What is out of scope

Authorship or amendment of the normative specifications. UN/CEFACT owns and governs UNTP, UNVTD, the
reference vocabularies and the Global Trust Registry, with its secretariat hosted by the United
Nations Economic Commission for Europe (UNECE). The World Customs Organization governs the WCO Data
Model. The World Wide Web Consortium (W3C) governs the Verifiable Credentials and Decentralised
Identifiers specifications. The Commons implements standards governed by others, holds no normative
authority over any of them, authors none of them, and coordinates with each through liaison on the
same terms.

Also out of scope is any activity that would grant a contributor ownership of, or exclusive rights
over, the common infrastructure.

## Three rules about specifications

These are the rules most likely to break under delivery pressure, which is why they are written down
rather than assumed.

**Nothing forks a specification, whoever owns it.** Schemas, contexts, vocabularies and registers are
referenced at their published location, never copied in. The failure this avoids is a widely used
community implementation becoming the de facto specification, after which there are two standards and
the normative one is the less used.

**A body's machine-readable artefacts belong at that body.** A vocabulary published as resolvable
linked data is the standard in another serialisation, carrying the same authority, and it belongs on
the body's own domain under its own identifiers. Prototyping one to make the case for publishing it
is legitimate and temporary by design: a named destination from the outset, identifier minting
configurable so nothing is rewritten at handover, and no operating role retained. A prototype with no
identified recipient is an orphan.

**Register authority is never the foundation's.** The authoritative trust, identity, conformance and
implementation registers are UN/CEFACT public goods. The Commons may operate one under revocable,
non-exclusive delegation, and may hold the code that serves a register and the configuration that
operates it. It never holds the data as its own asset. The same posture is offered to any other body,
since an exception made once is the one that gets cited.

## Why the name

The Commons is deliberately named for a commons rather than after any single standard. A government
agency, a standards body or a commercial organisation can support open digital trade infrastructure
without appearing to fund one standards owner through a proxy. The work already spans more than one
body: customs implementation depends on artefacts governed by UN/CEFACT and by the WCO and authored
by neither, so customs administrations, the WCO and UN/CEFACT are all constituencies of the same
organisation.

Although the UNECE secretariat that hosts UN/CEFACT carries a regional name, UN/CEFACT is a worldwide
standards body. The Commons is open on equal terms to governments, standards bodies and organisations
from every region, and is not a European undertaking.
